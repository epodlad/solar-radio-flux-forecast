What are we measuring?

The Sun sends out radio waves. Dedicated radio telescopes at the Dominion Radio Astrophysical Observatory in Penticton, Canada, make precise measurements of their strength at a wavelength of 10.7 centimetres. This daily measurement is called F10.7. It helps us follow changes in solar activity. We use the published Canadian measurements. About the observations.

The Canadian F10.7 record, now measured at Penticton, is a long-running, internationally used reference for solar activity. NOAA: the Canadian F10.7 record.

Why does this matter for Earth?

Solar ultraviolet radiation helps create free electrons in the ionosphere, a high layer of Earth’s atmosphere. TEC (Total Electron Content) describes how many electrons lie along a signal’s path, per unit area. It affects satellite navigation and radio signals.

F10.7 is an indicator of the Sun’s ultraviolet output, so it helps models estimate changes in the ionosphere. TEC also depends on location, time of day, season and geomagnetic activity. A forecast of F10.7 is useful input—not a TEC forecast by itself. F10.7 and ultraviolet radiation · About TEC.

Our forecast

This independent research service uses adaptive Kalman filtering to predict F10.7 for the next one to three days, with an estimate of uncertainty. These predictions may help studies and models that need advance estimates of solar activity. Their usefulness must be assessed by comparing forecasts with later measurements.

What does a Kalman filter do?

Imagine following a moving dot through a slightly blurry window. Each glimpse helps, but no glimpse is perfect. A Kalman filter combines what it expected with a new measurement to update its estimate. Our adaptive version also adjusts parts of the model as new data arrive.

  1. Expect: use earlier measurements to estimate what comes next.
  2. Compare: check how the new measurement differs from that estimate.
  3. Update: balance the two using their estimated uncertainties, then make a new forecast.

Signal means real changes in the Sun’s radio flux. Noise means measurement fluctuations—not sound. The filter helps reduce the influence of noise, but it cannot always tell noise from a sudden real change.

Two sources of uncertainty

Measurement noise: every observation has some uncertainty. Model noise: the real Sun changes in ways our simple model cannot fully describe.

The Kalman filter accounts for both when balancing its previous estimate with a new measurement. In our adaptive changing-trend model, the estimate of model noise is updated as observations arrive. Measurement uncertainty is set separately. This helps the model adjust to changing solar activity and estimate how uncertain its forecast is.

Why use it? It learns as measurements arrive and gives both a prediction and an estimate of uncertainty. It can still miss rapid changes. We check its forecasts against later observations to see how well it works.

Why use a second measurement? A later observation adds information about how the radio flux has changed since the main measurement. An experimental Kalman update can use it to refine the estimated flux while retaining the daily trend, and then revise the forecast and its uncertainty. More information does not guarantee a more accurate forecast: we need to test that against later observations. The service now accepts NOAA Afternoon observations. When a same-day afternoon observation is available, it produces a separate experimental Kalman correction. The noon forecasts remain available for comparison. Penticton also makes a morning measurement. The seasonal schedule reflects observing conditions, not daylight-saving time. Official measurement schedule.

Service checks: 20:45 and 23:45 UTC in March–October; 20:45 and 22:45 UTC in November–February, plus one check at startup. No five-minute data polling. Checks require the service to be running. If a publication is delayed, the previous forecast remains available until the next check.

Timing of the experimental update: NOAA time tags are preserved as published. NOAA afternoon tags can differ from the seasonal Penticton schedule above. The correction uses the NOAA-reported elapsed time; the physical measurement timing has not yet been reconciled.

Compare prediction errors

Smaller errors are better. “Keep the last value” is our simple comparison: tomorrow is predicted to be the same as today. A more complicated model must earn its place by doing better.

MAE is the average size of an error. RMSE gives bigger mistakes more weight. Bias shows whether predictions tend to be too high or too low. All three use sfu.

Errors are forecast minus observation. Bias is their average: positive means forecasts are too high on average, negative means too low. A near-zero bias can hide large errors of opposite signs. Skill is assessed against persistence on matching dates. Archived observations do not preserve historical publication times or revision vintages. Coverage is the fraction of measurements inside a prediction range; a well-calibrated 68% range should contain about 68% of measurements.

Technical details: the two models

Two sequential models learn from Penticton observations: a random walk with an exponentially smoothed drift, and a regression whose coefficients are updated by a Kalman filter. Forecasts extend the updated model to the next three noon observations.

Missing daily observations break the sequence and restart a 30-observation warmup. No future observations enter a forecast. Conditional Gaussian intervals omit some model and parameter uncertainty; empirical coverage must be checked before relying on them.

After a same-day evening Penticton measurement arrives, a separate experimental drift correction is shown. The two noon forecasts remain visible for comparison. The regression model is not re-trained on an intraday increment. This evening correction has not established forecast skill. One sfu = 10⁻²² W m⁻² Hz⁻¹.
